The ballot should say what your vote really does.

In a presidential election, the ballot usually feels like a direct vote for President. But the legal step is more indirect than that. Your vote helps decide which slate of electors your state will appoint. This website makes that plain.

Why this exists

The ballot is easy to use. It is not always honest about the system behind it.

Most voters are shown a ballot that looks more direct than the legal process really is. The Honest Ballot keeps the ballot readable while also showing the missing middle step.

It puts electors in view

Instead of hiding them in the background, it shows that the voter is choosing among slates of electors tied to each ticket.

It uses plain language

No legal fog. No civics-class softening. Just a direct explanation of what the vote does and does not do.

It shows the rule up front

The slate with the most votes statewide gets all of the state's electors. That rule should not be hidden.

EC basics

The key facts a voter should not have to hunt down.

Before a voter can judge the system, they need a few basic facts in plain language: how many electors there are, how a state gets them, and how many it takes to win.

There are 538 electors total

The country has 538 electoral votes in all. A candidate needs 270 to win the presidency.

Each state gets electors from Congress

A state's elector count equals its number of House seats plus 2 more for its Senate seats.

Arizona currently has 11

Arizona currently has 11 electors because it has 9 House seats and 2 Senate seats.

Washington, DC gets 3

DC is not a state, but it gets 3 electors under the Constitution.

Most states use winner-take-all

In most states, the statewide winner gets all of that state's electors.

Only two states split electors

Maine and Nebraska can split electoral votes instead of giving all of them to one ticket.

Why Arizona has 11 electors

9 House seats + 2 Senate seats = 11 electors
That is the number of electoral votes Arizona gets in a presidential election.

How many electors exist

50 states + DC = 538 electors
The whole Electoral College adds up to 538 electoral votes.

How many it takes to win

538 total ÷ 2, rounded up = 270
A presidential ticket needs 270 electoral votes to win.

Standard ballot

  • Electors are mostly invisible. The middle step is hidden or easy to miss.
  • The rule is often unstated. The voter may not be told that the statewide winner gets all electors.
  • The vote feels more direct than it is. The interface is simple, but it leaves out key facts.

Honest Ballot

What the voter should be able to see

  • The slate comes first. The ballot makes clear that the voter is choosing electors.
  • The electors are visible. The people who may actually cast the official votes are shown.
  • The rule is stated clearly. The voter is told that the statewide winner gets all electors.
  • The candidate names still appear. But they are shown as the ticket the slate is pledged to support, not as the whole legal story.

How it works

This is the process your vote enters.

The steps are simple. What is unusual is that the normal ballot does not spell them out.

Step 1

Each ticket has a slate of electors

Before Election Day, each presidential ticket is tied to a group of electors in the state.

Step 2

You pick one slate

You see candidate names on the ballot, but the legal choice attached to them is a slate of electors.

Step 3

The statewide winner gets all electors

In a winner-take-all state, the slate with the most votes statewide gets the whole set of electors.

Step 4

The electors cast the official votes

Those electors later cast the state's formal electoral votes for President and Vice President.

Counting rule
Plurality

Winner rule
Most votes wins

Winner award
All state electors
The statewide winner gets all 11 Arizona electors.

What happens after you vote

  1. You mark one slate.
  2. All votes in the state are counted.
  3. The slate with the most votes wins all 11 Arizona elector spots.
  4. Those 11 electors later cast Arizona's official votes for President and Vice President.
Important rule:
This ballot uses plurality voting. That means a slate can win all 11 electors without winning more than half of the vote. It only needs more votes than any other slate.

What votes for losing slates do not do

  1. They do not help appoint part of Arizona's elector slate.
  2. They do not add electors to the national total for that ticket.
  3. They are counted and reported, but they do not change who Arizona appoints unless that slate wins statewide.
Why this ballot looks different
Most ballots make the candidate feel like the direct object of the vote. This design puts the slate and the electors closer to the mark because that is the legal step the voter is actually taking.

This is a design prototype, not an official ballot. It is meant to show how a presidential ballot could be more direct about what the voter is legally doing.

Why it matters

A democratic system should not hide its own steps from the voter.

This project is not about making the ballot harder to read. It is about making the system easier to understand.

A ballot should not make voters feel they are doing one thing when the legal system is actually doing another. If electors are part of the process, the ballot should say that plainly.

Candidate names still matter. They help people find the choice they want. But they should not crowd out the fact that the legal choice on the ballot is a slate of electors.

Good civic design does not just help a person mark a box. It helps them understand what their mark means.
